---
title: "Problem 5.1 (Independence of mechanisms)"
---

**Problem 5.1 (Independence of mechanisms)** Let $P_X$ be the mixture of
$k$ sharp Gaussian peaks at positions $s_1,\dots,s_k$ as shown in Figure
5.5, left. Let $Y$ be obtained from $X$ by adding some Gaussian noise
$N$ with zero mean and a width $\sigma_N$ such that the separate peaks
remain visible as in Figure 5.5, right.

1.  Argue intuitively why $P_{X\mid Y}$ also contains information about
    the positions $s_1,\dots,s_k$ of the peaks and thus $P_{X\mid Y}$
    and $P_Y$ share this information.

2.  The transition between $P_X$ and $P_Y$ can be described by
    convolution (from $P_X$ to $P_Y$) and deconvolution (from $P_Y$ to
    $P_X$). If $P_{Y\mid X}$ is considered as the linear map converting
    the input $P_X$ to the output $P_Y$, then $P_{Y\mid X}$ coincides
    with the convolution map. Argue why $P_{X\mid Y}$ does *not*
    coincide with the deconvolution map (as one may think at first
    glance).

**Setup in formulas.**
$P_X = \sum_{i=1}^k w_i\,\mathcal N(s_i,\sigma_0^2)$ with weights
$w_i>0$, $\sum_i w_i = 1$, and $\sigma_0$ small; $Y = X + N$ with
$N\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_N^2)$, $N \perp\!\!\!\perp X$, and
$\sigma_0 \ll \sigma_N \ll \min_{i\ne j}|s_i-s_j|$.

**Definition (convolution).** For two probability measures $\mu,\nu$ on
$\mathbb R$, their convolution is the probability measure

$$
(\mu*\nu)(B) := \int_{\mathbb R}\int_{\mathbb R} \mathbf 1_B(x+z)\,\mu(dx)\,\nu(dz), \qquad B \in \mathcal B(\mathbb R).
$$

It is the law of $X+Z$ when $X\sim\mu$, $Z\sim\nu$, and
$X \perp\!\!\!\perp Z$ (by the product-measure/Fubini argument of Claim
1 in `anm_claims.pdf`). If $\mu,\nu$ have Lebesgue densities $p,q$, then
$\mu*\nu$ has density

$$
(p*q)(y) = \int_{\mathbb R} p(x)\,q(y-x)\,dx .
$$

Convolution is commutative, associative and linear in each argument. In
the problem, $P_Y = P_X * \mathcal N(0,\sigma_N^2)$, i.e.
$p_Y(y) = \int p_X(x)\,\varphi_{\sigma_N}(y-x)\,dx$ with
$\varphi_{\sigma}$ the $\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)$ density; the map
$p_X \mapsto p_X*\varphi_{\sigma_N}$ is linear and is exactly the kernel
$P_{Y\mid X=x} = \mathcal N(x,\sigma_N^2)$ integrated against $P_X$.

**Definition (deconvolution).** Deconvolution is the inverse problem:
given $q$ (the noise density) and the output $r = p*q$, recover the
input $p$. Formally it is the inverse of the linear map $p \mapsto p*q$,
whenever that inverse exists. With Fourier transforms
$\widehat{p}(t) := \int e^{itx}p(x)\,dx$ one has
$\widehat{p*q} = \widehat p\,\widehat q$, so

$$
p = \mathcal F^{-1}\big(\widehat r / \widehat q\big)
$$

provided
$\widehat q$ has no zeros. For Gaussian $q$,
$\widehat q(t) = e^{-\sigma_N^2 t^2/2}$ never vanishes, so the inverse
exists on a suitable class of functions, but it divides by an
exponentially small number at high frequencies: deconvolution is linear
but ill-posed (small perturbations of $r$ blow up). Note it is a fixed
linear map determined by $q$ alone; it does not depend on which $p$
produced $r$.

**Solution**

**a)** We have
$X \xrightarrow{}[] Y, Y = X + N, N \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_N^2)$.

**Solution a)** If we choose $y$ midway between two adjacent peaks, say
$s_1$ and $s_2$, then $P_{X\mid Y=y}$ puts its mass equally at $s_1$ and
$s_2$ with a gap in the middle, so we know that we are not uniquely near
any $s_i$. If we choose $y$ near $s_1$, then $P_{X\mid Y=y}$ has much
more mass around $s_1$. Reason:
$p(x\mid y) \propto p_X(x)\,\varphi_{\sigma_N}(y-x)$, and the factor
$p_X(x)$ is $\approx 0$ away from the $s_i$, so for every $y$ the mass
of $P_{X\mid Y=y}$ sits at the peak positions; $y$ only sets their
relative weights $\propto w_i\,\varphi_{\sigma_N}(y-s_i)$. Hence
$P_{X\mid Y}$ reveals $s_1,\dots,s_k$, just as $P_Y$ does, while
$P_{Y\mid X=x} = \mathcal N(x,\sigma_N^2)$ does not.
