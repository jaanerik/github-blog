---
title: "Problem 4.16 (Maximum likelihood)"
---

**Problem 4.16 (Maximum likelihood)** Assume that we are given an i.i.d.
data set $(X_1,Y_1),\dots,(X_n,Y_n)$ from the model

$$
Y = f(X) + N_Y, \quad \text{with } X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_X, \sigma_X^2), \text{ and } N_Y \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_{N_Y}, \sigma_{N_Y}^2) \text{ independent},
$$

where the function $f$ is supposed to be known.

1.  Prove that $f(x) = \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X = x]$.

2.  Write $\mathbf{x} := (x_1,\dots,x_n)$,
    $\mathbf{y} := (y_1,\dots,y_n)$ and consider the log-likelihood
    function

$$
\ell_\theta(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}) = \ell_\theta\big((x_1,y_1),\dots,(x_n,y_n)\big) = \sum_{i=1}^n \log p_\theta(x_i,y_i),
$$

    where $p_\theta$ is the joint density over $(X,Y)$ and
    $\theta := (\mu_X, \mu_{N_Y}, \sigma_X^2, \sigma_{N_Y}^2)$. Prove
    that for some $c_1, c_2 \in \mathbb{R}$ with $c_2 > 0$

$$
\max_\theta \ell_\theta(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}) = c_2 \cdot \big(c_1 - \log \widehat{\operatorname{var}}[\mathbf{x}] - \log \widehat{\operatorname{var}}[\mathbf{y} - f(\mathbf{x})]\big),
      \tag{4.20}
$$

where
    $\widehat{\operatorname{var}}[\mathbf{z}] := \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \big(z_i - \frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^n z_k\big)^2$
    estimates the variance.

Equation (4.20) motivates the comparison of expressions (4.18) and
(4.19):

$$
L_{X\to Y} = -\log \widehat{\operatorname{var}}[X] - \log \widehat{\operatorname{var}}[R_Y], \qquad
L_{X\leftarrow Y} = -\log \widehat{\operatorname{var}}[R_X] - \log \widehat{\operatorname{var}}[Y],
$$

with $R_Y := Y - \hat f_Y(X)$ and $R_X := X - \hat f_X(Y)$ the
regression residuals. The main difference is that in this exercise, we
have used the conditional mean and not the outcome of the regression
method.

**Solution a)** As stated this needs $\mu_{N_Y} = 0$: by linearity of
conditional expectation,

$$
\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X = x] = \mathbb{E}[f(X) \mid X = x] + \mathbb{E}[N_Y \mid X = x] = f(x) + \mu_{N_Y}.
$$

Why? Let us use Part A of `cond_exp.pdf`. Set
$\kappa_x := \delta_{f(x)}$. This is a regular conditional distribution
of $f(X)$ given $X$: (K1), (K2) are immediate, and (K3) holds since for
Borel $A,B$

$$
\int_A \delta_{f(x)}(B)\,P_X(dx) = \int_A \mathbf 1_{f^{-1}(B)}(x)\,P_X(dx) = P_X\big(A\cap f^{-1}(B)\big) = P\big(X\in A,\ f(X)\in B\big).
$$

Hence we can write

$$
E\left[f(X) \mid X = x\right] = \int z \delta_{f(x)}(dz) = f(x).
$$

And the second equals the unconditional mean $\mathbb{E}[N_Y]$ because
$N_Y \perp\!\!\!\perp X$, because of lemma 2 (note that the independence
is used in second to last equation in the equation chain). So
$f(x) = \mathbb{E}[Y\mid X=x]$ iff $\mu_{N_Y}=0$. This is a harmless
normalization: replacing $(f,\mu_{N_Y})$ by $(\tilde f, 0)$ with
$\tilde f := f + \mu_{N_Y}$ gives the identical joint distribution, so
only the sum $f + \mu_{N_Y}$ — that is, the regression function
$x \mapsto \mathbb{E}[Y\mid X=x]$ — is identifiable.

**Solution b)** The joint density factorizes as
$p_\theta(x,y) = p(x)\,p(y\mid x)$ with
$X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_X,\sigma_X^2)$ and
$Y \mid X = x \sim \mathcal{N}(f(x)+\mu_{N_Y},\, \sigma_{N_Y}^2)$.

Why does the conditional distribution look like that? Left as a gap.

Writing $r_k := y_k - f(x_k)$,

$$
\ell_\theta(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}) = \sum_{k=1}^n \left[ -\tfrac12\log(2\pi\sigma_X^2) - \frac{(x_k-\mu_X)^2}{2\sigma_X^2} \right] + \sum_{k=1}^n \left[ -\tfrac12\log(2\pi\sigma_{N_Y}^2) - \frac{(r_k-\mu_{N_Y})^2}{2\sigma_{N_Y}^2} \right] =: \ell_\theta(\mathbf{x}) + \ell_\theta(\mathbf{r}).
$$

Note the $-\tfrac{n}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2)$ terms are *not* constant in
$\theta$; without them the $\sigma^2$-derivatives would be strictly
positive and have no critical point. Note also that $\mu_{N_Y}$ must
stay free: $\theta$ has four components, and maximizing over the slice
$\mu_{N_Y}=0$ is a different (smaller) number in general.

The two blocks have the same form, so it suffices to maximize one, say
$\ell_\theta(\mathbf{x})$ over $(\mu_X, \sigma_X^2)$. First-order
conditions:

$$
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \mu_X} = \sum_k \frac{x_k - \mu_X}{\sigma_X^2} = 0
\;\Rightarrow\; \hat\mu_X = \bar x := \tfrac1n \textstyle\sum_k x_k,
$$

$$
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \sigma_X^2} = -\frac{n}{2\sigma_X^2} + \sum_k \frac{(x_k-\mu_X)^2}{2\sigma_X^4} = 0
\;\Rightarrow\; \hat\sigma_X^2 = \tfrac1n \textstyle\sum_k (x_k - \bar x)^2 = \widehat{\operatorname{var}}[\mathbf{x}].
$$

(This critical point is the maximum, because the density funciton is
smooth and can be checked by having the Hessian minors’ determinants
have signs -1, 1. Then we can check that it is a global min)
Substituting back, the quadratic term collapses to a constant:

$$
\sum_k \frac{(x_k-\bar x)^2}{2\,\widehat{\operatorname{var}}[\mathbf{x}]} = \frac{n\,\widehat{\operatorname{var}}[\mathbf{x}]}{2\,\widehat{\operatorname{var}}[\mathbf{x}]} = \frac n2,
$$

so

$$
\max_{\mu_X,\sigma_X^2} \ell_\theta(\mathbf{x}) = -\frac n2 \log \widehat{\operatorname{var}}[\mathbf{x}] - \frac n2 \log(2\pi) - \frac n2 .
$$

The residual block is identical with $r_k$ in place of $x_k$:
$\hat\mu_{N_Y} = \bar r$,
$\hat\sigma_{N_Y}^2 = \widehat{\operatorname{var}}[\mathbf{y}-f(\mathbf{x})]$
— the free $\mu_{N_Y}$ is exactly what centers the residuals, producing
the *centered* $\widehat{\operatorname{var}}$ rather than
$\tfrac1n\sum_k r_k^2$: the $\sigma^2$-condition is
$\hat\sigma_{N_Y}^2 = \tfrac1n\sum_k (r_k-\mu_{N_Y})^2$; with
$\mu_{N_Y}$ free, $\hat\mu_{N_Y}=\bar r$ and this is
$\widehat{\operatorname{var}}[\mathbf r]$; with $\mu_{N_Y}$ fixed at
$\mu_0$ it would be
$\widehat{\operatorname{var}}[\mathbf r] + (\bar r-\mu_0)^2$ and (4.20)
would fail as stated. Adding and factoring out $n/2$:

$$
\max_\theta \ell_\theta(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}) = \frac n2\Big( \underbrace{-2\log(2\pi) - 2}_{c_1} - \log\widehat{\operatorname{var}}[\mathbf{x}] - \log\widehat{\operatorname{var}}[\mathbf{y}-f(\mathbf{x})] \Big),
$$

which is (4.20) with $c_2 = n/2 > 0$ and $c_1 = -2(\log 2\pi + 1)$. The
coefficient 1 on each log term pins $c_2 = n/2$ uniquely — $(c_1,c_2)$
cannot be rescaled to, say, $(n, -\log 2\pi - 1)$ (why?). Since
$c_1, c_2$ do not depend on the direction of the model, comparing causal
directions by maximum likelihood reduces to comparing
$\log\widehat{\operatorname{var}}[\mathbf{x}] + \log\widehat{\operatorname{var}}[\mathbf{y}-f(\mathbf{x})]$
with its flipped counterpart, i.e. the scores (4.18)/(4.19).
