**Problem 3.6**. Recall Eq (3.4):

$$
P_C^{\mathcal{C}; do(E:=2)} = \mathcal{N}(0,1) \ne P^{\mathcal{C}}_{C | E=2},
$$

where $C:=N_C, E:= 4C + N_E$ and $N_C, N_E \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$.

Show that $P^{\mathcal{C}}_{C | E=2}$ is Gaussian:

$$
C | E = 2 \sim \mathcal{N}(8/17, \sigma^2 = 1/17)
$$

Per Bayes’ theorem $p(C|E=2) = p(E=2 | C) p(C) / p(E=2)$, and our goal is to massage the right-hand side into the standard Gaussian form

$$
p(C | E=2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2 \pi}\,\sigma} \exp\left( - \frac{(C-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} \right), \qquad \mu = 8/17,\ \sigma^2 = 1/17.
$$

**(0)** First let us remind ourselves how the change of variables work.

When we have a continuous measure $\mu \ll \mathrm{Leb}$ then we can write $\int d\mu = \int p(x) dx$ where $p(x)$ is the density function with respect to the Lebesgue measure. We can write $F(x) := P(X < x)$. Now let us look at some transformation $g: X \mapsto g(X)$ and we care about $G(x) := P(g(X) < x)$.

Let $(S, \Sigma)$ be measurable and $h: S \xrightarrow{} \mathbb{R}$ function and $\mathcal{B} := \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})$. We say that $h$ is $\Sigma-$measurable or $\Sigma \setminus \mathcal{B}$-measurable if every Borel set has it’s original in $\Sigma$.

Now let us look at measure spaces $(S, \Sigma, \mu)$ and $(S', \Sigma')$. Let us define a generalisation for $\mu h^{-1}$.

Let us have a function $T: S \xrightarrow{} S'$ such that $T^{-1} \in \Sigma$ for every $B \in \Sigma'$. Now define

$$
\mu T^{-1}: \Sigma' \xrightarrow{} [0,\infty], \mu T^{-1}(A') = \mu(T^{-1} A').
$$

If $(S',\Sigma') = (\mathbb{R}, \mathcal{B})$ then $\mu T^{-1} = \mu h^{-1}$. If $f \in m \Sigma'^{+}$, then for every $A' \in \Sigma'$ we have

$$
\int_{T^{-1}A'}{f(Ts)\mu(ds)} = \int_{A'}{f(s') \mu T^{-1} (ds')} \text{  (*) and}
$$

$$
f \in \mathcal{L}_1(S', \Sigma', \mu T^{-1}) \text{ iff } f \circ T \in \mathcal{L}_1 (S, \Sigma, \mu) \text{ and equation (*) holds.}
$$

Now specialise this machinery to densities — which is all the “Jacobian” ever was. Let $X$ be a random variable on $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ with law $P_X := \mathbb{P} X^{-1}$ on $(\mathbb{R}, \mathcal{B})$, and suppose $P_X$ has a density $p_X$ with respect to Lebesgue measure $\lambda$, i.e. $dP_X = p_X\, d\lambda$. Take $T: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ a $C^1$ diffeomorphism and set $Y := T(X)$. Pushforwards compose, so the law of $Y$ is

$$
P_Y = \mathbb{P} Y^{-1} = \mathbb{P}(T \circ X)^{-1} = (\mathbb{P} X^{-1}) T^{-1} = P_X T^{-1}.
$$

For any $A' \in \mathcal{B}$, the image-measure identity (\*) with $f = \mathbf{1}_{A'}$ gives

$$
P_Y(A') = P_X(T^{-1} A') = \int_{\mathbb{R}} \mathbf{1}_{A'}(Tx)\, p_X(x)\, \lambda(dx).
$$

So far there is **no Jacobian**: the abstract change of variables (\*) is exact for any measurable $T$. The factor enters only when we re-express this integral against $\lambda$ itself, via the substitution $y = Tx$. That substitution is not imported from calculus as a black box — it is justified, within this same framework, by the following lemma.

**Lemma (density of the pushforward of Lebesgue measure).** For a $C^1$ diffeomorphism $T : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, the pushforward $\lambda T^{-1}$ is absolutely continuous with respect to $\lambda$, with

$$
\frac{d(\lambda T^{-1})}{d\lambda}(y) = \bigl|(T^{-1})'(y)\bigr|, \qquad \text{i.e.} \qquad (\lambda T^{-1})(dy) = \bigl|(T^{-1})'(y)\bigr|\, \lambda(dy).
$$

*Proof.* Take $T$ increasing (the decreasing case is identical up to a sign). For an interval $A' = (c, d]$ we have $T^{-1} A' = (T^{-1}c,\, T^{-1}d]$, so by the fundamental theorem of calculus (valid since $T^{-1} \in C^1$),

$$
(\lambda T^{-1})(A') = \lambda(T^{-1} A') = T^{-1}(d) - T^{-1}(c) = \int_c^d (T^{-1})'(y)\, dy = \int_{A'} \bigl|(T^{-1})'\bigr|\, d\lambda.
$$

For $T$ decreasing the endpoints swap and $(T^{-1})' < 0$, which is exactly what produces the absolute value. Both sides are measures in $A'$ agreeing on the $\pi$-system of intervals, hence agree on all of $\mathcal{B}$ by uniqueness of measures. $\;\square$

Now combine the two. Apply (\*) with $f = g \circ T^{-1}$, so that $f(Tx) = g(T^{-1}Tx) = g(x)$:

$$
\int_{T^{-1} A'} g(x)\, \lambda(dx) = \int_{A'} g(T^{-1}y)\, (\lambda T^{-1})(dy) = \int_{A'} g(T^{-1}y)\, \bigl|(T^{-1})'(y)\bigr|\, \lambda(dy),
$$

Trying to derive the first equation in prev eq.

$$
\int_{T^{-1}A'} g(x) \lambda (dx) = \int_{A'} = g(T^{-1} y) \lambda T^{-1}(dy)
$$

the last equality by the lemma. This **is** the substitution rule $\lambda(dx) = |(T^{-1})'(y)|\, \lambda(dy)$ in differential shorthand; the only analytic input was the fundamental theorem of calculus applied to $\lambda T^{-1}$ on intervals. Taking $g = p_X$ turns our expression for the law of $Y$ into

$$
P_Y(A') = \int_{A'} p_X(T^{-1} y)\, \bigl|(T^{-1})'(y)\bigr|\, \lambda(dy).
$$

Since this holds for every $A' \in \mathcal{B}$, uniqueness of the Radon–Nikodym derivative identifies the density of $Y$:

$$
p_Y(y) = \frac{dP_Y}{d\lambda}(y) = p_X(T^{-1} y)\, \bigl|(T^{-1})'(y)\bigr|.
$$

The factor $|(T^{-1})'(y)|$ is precisely the Radon–Nikodym derivative $\frac{d(\lambda T^{-1})}{d\lambda}$ — the density with which $T$ distorts Lebesgue measure. That is the whole content of the word “Jacobian”: not a separate rule for densities, but the price of measuring against a $\lambda$ that $T$ does not preserve.

For the affine map $T(x) = ax + b$ (with $a \ne 0$) we have $T^{-1}(y) = (y-b)/a$ and $|(T^{-1})'(y)| = 1/|a|$, so

$$
p_{aX+b}(y) = \frac{1}{|a|}\, p_X\!\Big(\frac{y-b}{a}\Big).
$$

With $a = 4$, $b = 0$ this is the $p_{4X}(x) = \tfrac14 p_X(x/4)$ used next; for $X \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$ it returns $\mathcal{N}(b, a^2)$.

**(1)** Recall the convolution operation. For standard normal rvs X,Y we have

$$
p(X+Y = x) = \int da \ p(X=a) p(Y = x-a) = (\frac{1}{\sqrt{2 \pi} \sigma_X \sigma_Y}) \exp{-\frac{(a-\mu_X)^2}{2\sigma_X^2}}\exp{-\frac{(x-a-\mu_Y)^2}{2\sigma_Y^2}}
$$

X+Y should have $\mu = \mu_X + \mu_Y$ and $\sigma^2 = \sigma_X^2 + \sigma_Y^2$

Let it be standard normal so we simplify to

$$
p(X+Y = x) = \int da \ p(X=a) p(Y = x-a) = \int da \ \frac{1}{2 \pi} \exp{(-\frac{a^2}{2})}\exp{(-\frac{(x-a)^2}{2})}
$$

Complete the square in $a$ to separate the $x$ part from the integration variable:

$$
-\frac{1}{2}\left[a^2 + (x-a)^2\right] = -\left[a^2 - ax + \frac{x^2}{2}\right] = -(a - \frac{x}{2})^2 - \frac{x^2}{4}
$$

so

$$
p(X+Y = x) = \frac{1}{2 \pi} \exp{(-\frac{x^2}{4})} \int da \ \exp{(-(a - \frac{x}{2})^2)}
$$

The remaining integral is a plain Gaussian, $\int da \ \exp{(-(a-\frac{x}{2})^2)} = \sqrt{\pi}$, independent of $x$. Hence

$$
p(X+Y = x) = \frac{1}{2 \pi} \sqrt{\pi} \exp{(-\frac{x^2}{4})} = \frac{1}{2 \sqrt{\pi}} \exp{(-\frac{x^2}{4})} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2 \pi \cdot 2}} \exp{(-\frac{x^2}{2 \cdot 2})}
$$

which is exactly $X + Y \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 2)$, confirming $\mu = \mu_X + \mu_Y = 0$ and $\sigma^2 = \sigma_X^2 + \sigma_Y^2 = 2$.

**(2)** Apply the affine rule from (0) with $a = 4$, $b = 0$:

$$
p(4X = x) = \frac{1}{4} p(X=x/4) = \frac{1}{4} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2 \pi} \sigma} \exp{\left( -\frac{(x/4-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2 \pi} (4\sigma)} \exp{\left( -\frac{(x-4\mu)^2}{2 (4\sigma)^2}\right)}
$$

which is $\mathcal{N}(4\mu, (4\sigma)^2) = \mathcal{N}(4\mu, 16\sigma^2)$: the $\frac{1}{4} = |(T^{-1})'|$ from (0) turns the prefactor into $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}(4\sigma)}$, exactly the normalization for standard deviation $4\sigma$.

Now assemble the three pieces. Since $E = 4C + N_E$ with $N_E \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$, conditioning on $C$ shifts the mean by $4C$, so $E \mid C \sim \mathcal{N}(4C, 1)$. The prior is $C \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$, and the evidence $E = 4C + N_E \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 16+1)$ is a plain number (here the $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{17}}$ is the $a=\sqrt{17}$ factor from the affine rule in (0)):

$$
p(E=2\mid C) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\!\left(-\tfrac12(2-4C)^2\right), \qquad p(C) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\!\left(-\tfrac12 C^2\right),
$$

$$
p(E=2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\cdot 17}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{2^2}{2\cdot 17}\right) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{34\pi}}\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{2}{17}\right).
$$

Multiply likelihood and prior and complete the square in $C$:

$$
p(E=2\mid C)\,p(C) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\exp\!\left(-\tfrac12\left(17C^2 - 16C + 4\right)\right),
$$

and since $17C^2 - 16C + 4 = 17\left(C - \tfrac{8}{17}\right)^2 + \tfrac{4}{17}$ (using $17 \cdot \tfrac{64}{289} = \tfrac{64}{17}$ and $4 - \tfrac{64}{17} = \tfrac{4}{17}$),

$$
p(E=2\mid C)\,p(C) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{17}{2}\left(C - \tfrac{8}{17}\right)^2\right)\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{2}{17}\right).
$$

Now divide by $p(E=2)$ — the algebra closes on its own, no appeal to “it must be a normalizer.” The $\exp(-2/17)$ factors cancel exactly, and the prefactor collapses:

$$
\frac{1/(2\pi)}{1/\sqrt{34\pi}} = \frac{\sqrt{34\pi}}{2\pi} = \frac{\sqrt{34}}{2\sqrt{\pi}} = \frac{\sqrt{17}}{\sqrt{2\pi}},
$$

so

$$
p(C \mid E=2) = \frac{\sqrt{17}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{17}{2}\left(C - \tfrac{8}{17}\right)^2\right) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\cdot\tfrac{1}{17}}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\left(C - \tfrac{8}{17}\right)^2}{2\cdot\tfrac{1}{17}}\right).
$$

This is exactly the density of $\mathcal{N}\!\left(\tfrac{8}{17},\ \tfrac{1}{17}\right)$, correctly normalized by direct computation — the dividing $p(E=2)$ supplies precisely the $\sqrt{17}/\sqrt{2\pi}$ that a $\sigma^2 = 1/17$ Gaussian requires.
